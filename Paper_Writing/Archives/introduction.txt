**Introduction**

Adaptive navigation of multirobot systems through physical environments has successfully been applied to search and rescue and environmental monitoring missions. In most cases, these environments are modeled as scalar fields representing quantities like temperature or chemical concentration. Control primitives based on gradient methods enable robots to find extrema, saddle points, ridges, or isolines within these fields. These scalar field approaches have proven effective for many applications where the environment can be characterized by a single value at each location.

However, many physical phenomena cannot be adequately represented as scalar fields. Ocean currents, atmospheric flows, and electromagnetic fields inherently possess both magnitude and direction at each point. Modeling these environments as vector fields reveals topological features such as vortices and separatrices that have no scalar field equivalent. The directional information in vector fields creates complex dynamics near critical points where the field magnitude approaches zero. These critical points include sources, sinks, saddles, and vortices that each exhibit distinct local behavior determined by both rotational and divergent field components.

Consider a team of underwater robots monitoring an oceanic vortex or atmospheric drones tracking a developing cyclone. These robots must maintain safe distances from dangerous field centers while gathering crucial data. Current approaches to this problem rely on a fundamental simplification. They flatten the vector field into a scalar representation by extracting magnitude or divergence, then apply gradient descent or direction following strategies. Previous work has shown that robots can follow the direction toward a sink or center of a vortex. Yet this dimensionality reduction discards the geometric information that distinguishes different critical point types, and requires prior knowledge of the vector field to be successful.
The limitations become particularly apparent when robots attempt to maintain a set distance from critical points. Existing methods provide the general direction toward these centers but cannot determine their actual locations. When implemented on physical platforms, momentum effects cause robots attempting to orbit a critical point to spiral outward. The controller cannot compensate for drift without knowing where the field center lies. Even with multiple robots sharing information, current distributed strategies pool directional measurements or magnitude gradients without reconstructing the underlying field structure. These approaches typically require memory of past measurements or global field maps, limiting their applicability when field characteristics change during operation.

This paper introduces a distributed estimation and control strategy that exploits local field geometry rather than reducing dimensionality. Our approach uses instantaneous vector measurements from multiple robots to reconstruct the local Jacobian matrix of the field. The key insight is that three robots taking simultaneous measurements at known positions provide sufficient information to estimate the local linear approximation of the field. This minimal team size can fully characterize planar field geometry near a critical point. The reconstructed Jacobian reveals both the critical point location and its type through eigenvalue analysis.
Building on this estimation capability, we develop control primitives for precise navigation around each critical point type. Robots can compute exact distances to field centers and adjust their trajectories accordingly. This enables stable orbit control at specified radii, precise approach trajectories to sources, and controlled avoidance of sinks. The method operates entirely on instantaneous measurements without memory requirements, making it suitable for time varying fields where critical points move or change type during operation.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we present a distributed algorithm that estimates critical point locations and types using only instantaneous vector measurements from three or more robots. Second, we develop control strategies that exploit these estimates to achieve precise navigation behaviors around different critical point types. Third, we validate our approach through extensive hardware experiments and simulations, demonstrating centimeter level accuracy in a two meter square testbed.

Experimental results show that robots maintain orbit radii within two centimeters of their targets despite sensing noise and actuation errors. The approach scales beyond the minimum three robot configuration, with additional robots improving estimation accuracy without requiring algorithmic modifications. Through systematic trials across six canonical field families, we demonstrate reliable performance when communication delays and sensor noise affect the system.
