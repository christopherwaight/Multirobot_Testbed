\chapter{First-Order Primitives: Critical Points}
\label{ch:first_order}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:ch6:intro}

% New text
Chapter~\ref{ch:zeroth} showed that direction-only primitives reach a
feature only when the field itself carries the cluster there. Orbiting a
fixed vortex with the vector-sum primitive drifted steadily outward,
because nothing in a heading measures the distance to the center. This
chapter moves one rung up the ladder of Section~\ref{sec:ch4:ladder}.

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
Recovering those coordinates is harder than following a heading, for reasons of both information and implementation. The information problem is that the location and classification of a critical point are absent from any single velocity sample; they are carried by the spatial derivatives of the field \cite{54}. A single platform must move, sample again, and difference its readings, which conflates the spatial gradient with the field's change in time and can return features that are not physically present. A distributed team removes this temporal aliasing by sampling at one instant \cite{28,29,44}, but recovering the derivatives is still not recovering the feature. Magnitude-based methods \cite{26} reduce the flow to a scalar and yield a descent direction, but the magnitude vanishes at every critical point regardless of type, and estimating the two velocity components as independent scalar fields still does not locate the point. Both the coordinate and the identity are properties of the assembled Jacobian. Inverting it recovers the location where the field vanishes, and its eigenvalues determine the type \cite{9}.

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
The implementation problem is that the local field must be reconstructed on physical hardware from noisy distributed measurements. The conditioning of this reconstruction degrades as the formation approaches collinearity, so the sampling shape must be held by a formation controller under real actuator dynamics and communication latency. Both the estimator and the mechatronics of the cluster limit the accuracy of the recovered features.

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
This chapter develops a distributed cooperative estimation strategy that recovers the local linear structure of the field. Using a minimum of three robots, the cluster estimates the local Jacobian in a single time step and treats critical point navigation as a local-to-global inference. Inverting the Jacobian predicts the location of the nearby critical point that organizes the surrounding flow, and its eigenvalues fix the identity, with no global map required. The same estimate supports two control laws, a proportional law that drives the cluster centroid onto the critical point, and an orbital law that holds the centroid at a commanded standoff radius.

\section{Critical Point Estimation from Three Robots}
\label{sec:ch6:estimation}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
Given a cluster of three robots sampling a planar vector field with robot positions $\mathbf{p}_i = (x_i, y_i)$ and corresponding vector field measurements $(u_i, v_i)$ for $i \in \{1, 2, 3\}$, we can approximate the vector field components across the cluster's spatial extent using linear planar models:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch6:2}
U(x,y) = ax + by + c
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch6:3}
V(x,y) = dx + ey + f
\end{equation}

The coefficients are determined by solving the linear systems:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch6:4}
\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}_u = \mathbf{u}, \quad \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta}_v = \mathbf{v}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch6:5}
\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\ x_2 & y_2 & 1 \\ x_3 & y_3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{\theta}_u = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{bmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{\theta}_v = \begin{bmatrix} d \\ e \\ f \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
and $\mathbf{u} = [u_1, u_2, u_3]^T$, $\mathbf{v} = [v_1, v_2, v_3]^T$ are the collected field measurements.

The critical point $\mathbf{p}^* = (x^*, y^*)$ satisfies $U(x^*, y^*) = 0$ and $V(x^*, y^*) = 0$, which in matrix form reads $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{p}^* = -\mathbf{h}$, where the Jacobian matrix and offset vector are:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch6:8}
\mathbf{J} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ d & e \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{h} = \begin{bmatrix} c \\ f \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Thus, provided $\det(\mathbf{J}) \neq 0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch6:9}
\mathbf{p}^* = -\mathbf{J}^{-1}\mathbf{h}
\end{equation}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
The eigenvalues of the Jacobian can be used to identify the type of nearby critical point (Table~\ref{tab:ch3:field_summary}).

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
If the true field is affine over the cluster's spatial extent, the models (\ref{eq:ch6:2})--(\ref{eq:ch6:3}) reproduce it without error and the recovery is exact at any formation size. On a smooth nonlinear field, the fit carries a first-order linearization error that scales with the local curvature of the field; this error decreases as the formation contracts toward the critical point.

\subsection{Minimum Robot Requirements}
\label{sec:ch6:minimum}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
Equations (\ref{eq:ch6:2})--(\ref{eq:ch6:3}) each contain three coefficients. From (\ref{eq:ch6:4}), each robot contributes one equation to each system: robot $i$ provides $u_i = ax_i + by_i + c$ for the U-component and $v_i = dx_i + ey_i + f$ for the V-component. To uniquely determine three unknowns requires at least three equations. Therefore: three robots are the minimum required for critical point estimation.

With fewer than three robots, the system becomes underdetermined: the matrix $\mathbf{A}$ in (\ref{eq:ch6:4}) has fewer rows than columns. This admits infinitely many solutions for the coefficients, preventing unique identification of the critical point location. With more than three robots ($n > 3$), the system becomes overdetermined and is solved via least-squares minimization.

Three non-collinear robots are also sufficient: $\det(\mathbf{A})$ equals twice the signed area of the formation triangle, so $\mathbf{A}$ is invertible exactly when the robots are not collinear.

The estimate named the class of both fields correctly in effectively
every cycle, including cycles more than 0.8~m from the critical point.
Every vortex error is a stable spiral with a small negative real part,
and none is a saddle or a node. A small damping in the printed map or a
small bias in the fit is enough to turn a center into a weak spiral, and
the data cannot separate the two.

Classification carries one condition that location does not. A
reflection of the sensed vectors reverses the sign of
$\det\hat{\mathbf{J}}$, which exchanges saddles with nodes but leaves the
estimated critical point where it was. The two printed maps were placed
under different frame conventions, including reflections, rotations, and
camera mirroring. The results above use the registration from the field
reconstructions of Section~\ref{sec:ch2:reconstruction}, which negates
the sensed $v$ component for the saddle map only. The controller applied
no registration, since it used the estimate only for location, and its
unregistered Jacobian read the saddle map as a stable node or spiral
throughout the terminal window. A deployment that reports feature type
therefore needs its sensor frame registered to its navigation frame.

\section{Attraction Primitive}
\label{sec:ch6:attraction}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
We develop two control primitives that drive the cluster centroid $\mathbf{p}_c = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbf{p}_i$ toward or around the estimated critical point $\mathbf{p}^*$. The mapping from cluster-level velocity commands to individual robot velocities is handled by the cluster space controller of Chapter~\ref{ch:estimation}.

\subsection{Definition}
\label{sec:ch6:attraction_def}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
To navigate the cluster center $\mathbf{p}_c$ to the critical point, we apply a proportional control law:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch6:10}
\dot{\mathbf{p}}_c = k(\mathbf{p}^* - \mathbf{p}_c)
\end{equation}
where $k > 0$ is the proportional gain. This linear error dynamic guarantees global exponential convergence to the estimated critical point with time constant $\tau = 1/k$ provided the estimate is stationary.

\subsection{Simulation Verification}
\label{sec:ch6:attraction_sim}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
A Python-based simulation implementing the three-layer control architecture of Chapter~\ref{ch:estimation} was developed to verify the critical point estimation and control primitives of this chapter. All simulation trials used a cluster of three robots in an equilateral triangular formation with a 0.33~m side length, matching the configuration used for hardware validation (Section~\ref{sec:ch6:attraction_hw}), with control gains of $k = 1$~s$^{-1}$ in (\ref{eq:ch6:10}), and $k_t = 0.2$~m/s, $k_r = 0.5$~s$^{-1}$ in (\ref{eq:ch6:11}).

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
Six noise-free analytical fields, one for each of the canonical critical point types listed in Table~\ref{tab:ch3:field_summary}, were used for trials (equations for these fields are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:fields}). After performance in noise-free environments was verified, simulations were performed on two fields reconstructed from robot-sensed measurements collected on the hardware testbed (vortex and saddle). These reconstructed fields incorporate realistic sensor noise from the physical testbed. Section~\ref{sec:ch2:reconstruction} describes how the reconstructed fields were built.

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
To test convergence to critical points, the following simulation experiments were performed. For each of the eight fields, the cluster was initialized at a random position drawn uniformly from a 1~m $\times$ 1~m box centered on the true critical point and the simulation was run for 100 time steps at 10~Hz. A trial was counted as convergent if the trajectory remained bounded and terminated within the stiction-limited neighborhood of the critical point; the final-position statistics quantify that neighborhood. \section{Orbital Primitive}
\label{sec:ch6:orbital}

\subsection{Definition}
\label{sec:ch6:orbital_def}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
To maintain a circular orbit of radius $r_d$ around $\mathbf{p}^*$, we define the radial vector $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{p}^* - \mathbf{p}_c$ with magnitude $r = \|\mathbf{r}\|$. The unit radial vector is $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{r}/r$, and the tangent vector $\hat{\mathbf{r}}_\perp$ is obtained via a $-90^\circ$ rotation. The orbital velocity command is:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch6:11}
\dot{\mathbf{p}}_c = k_t \hat{\mathbf{r}}_\perp + k_r(r - r_d)\hat{\mathbf{r}}
\end{equation}

Here, $k_t$ determines the orbital speed (where $\text{sgn}(k_t)$ dictates direction) and $k_r > 0$ drives radial convergence.
This control law decouples radial and angular dynamics. The radial error $e_r = r - r_d$ follows $\dot{e}_r = -k_r e_r$, ensuring exponential convergence to $r_d$. As $r \to r_d$, the angular velocity stabilizes to $\omega = k_t/r_d$, producing steady circular motion.

\section{Comparison with Zeroth-Order Primitives}
\label{sec:ch6:comparison}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
The modular architecture allows navigation controllers to be interchanged while cluster and robot controller layers remain fixed. As a result, performance differences between controllers reflect algorithmic rather than implementation variations.

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
We evaluate orbital control by comparing our orbital controller against two alternatives in the vortex field. The vector-sum approach commands the cluster to move in the direction of the average perceived flow. The vector-to-scalar primitive \cite{26} reduces the flow to its magnitude; here it was modified to move tangentially to the direction of steepest descent.

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
As can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch6:primitive_comparison}, both alternative primitives exhibit outward spiral drift in the vortex field. This is because both approaches derive their velocity commands from direction-only guidance; neither has a mechanism to detect or correct radial error with respect to a specific point, and so centrifugal drift goes uncorrected. The vector-sum drift has been confirmed in hardware in~\cite{28} and Chapter~\ref{ch:zeroth}. In the saddle field the comparison is more decisive: the field vectors do not all point toward the critical point, so the vector-sum controller cannot produce an orbital trajectory at all, and the vector-to-scalar technique would still drift.

\section{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:ch6:error}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
Estimation and control errors arise from four sources: robot dynamics, sensor noise, cluster geometry, and field conditioning.

Perfect convergence does not occur in simulation. However, repeating experiments with no stiction, no max velocity, and $\alpha = 0$ (instantaneous velocity response) confirms convergence to the controller's $10^{-6}$~m command deadband, with the critical point estimate itself exact to machine precision. This result confirms the variance is due to robot dynamics, not the estimation method. A nonlinear controller with gain scheduling could overcome these dynamics, but the linear controller proposed serves as a baseline. Some errors may also have arisen from modeling discrepancies such as modeling all robots identically with the average stiction, max speed, and response time values measured in the robots.

Positive radial bias at small orbital radii is also a result of robot dynamics. At small radii, the commanded angular rate $k_t / r_d$ is high relative to the radial correction bandwidth $k_r$, and the centripetal demand ($k_t^2 / r$) pushes the cluster outward faster than the radial controller can correct. A point-mass replication of the robot dynamics reproduces the simulated bias column of Table~\ref{tab:ch6:simulation_orbital_results} to within 0.001~m and attributes roughly 80\% of the small-radius bias to the first-order actuator lag and the remainder to the 10~Hz discretization of the tangential step. As the commanded radius increases, this effect diminishes. More aggressive radial gains would reduce this error, at the cost of increased sensitivity to noise in the critical point estimate.

Comparing the noise-free and reconstructed fields in simulation isolates the effect of noisy sensor data: sensor noise appears to be the driving factor of the simulation-reality gap, with sensing errors propagating directly through the critical point estimation equations. Nonetheless, the robot cluster detected and converged to the critical point, confirming the effectiveness of multirobot critical point estimation on physical robots. To first order, the centered equilateral fit maps per-component sensor noise $\sigma_v$ into estimate scatter $\sigma_p \approx \|\mathbf{J}^{-1}\|\,\sigma_v/\sqrt{3}$; the calibration errors of Section~\ref{sec:ch2:calibration} give $\sigma_v \approx 0.02$~m/s, predicting roughly 0.03~m of scatter on the printed vortex. This matches the observed 0.009--0.016~m precision after closed-loop time-averaging, while the 0.012--0.028~m bias reflects systematic distortion that averaging cannot remove.

To avoid errors arising from robots becoming collinear (the condition number of $\mathbf{A}$ becomes infinite), an equilateral triangle was chosen as the cluster shape. This shape minimizes the condition number of $\mathbf{A}$ in (\ref{eq:ch6:4}) among formations of the same size centered on the sampled region, making this formation optimal for sampling.

Further errors could arise from poor conditioning of the estimated Jacobian $\kappa(\mathbf{J})$ in (\ref{eq:ch6:8}), directly affecting the estimation of the critical point location (\ref{eq:ch6:9}). The negative bias in orbital performance at the largest commanded radii traces to degradation of the reconstructed field near the edge of the mapped region: there the estimated critical point distance is systematically inflated, so the radial controller pulls the cluster inside the commanded radius. This is the range-dependent error amplification captured by $\kappa(\mathbf{J})$, which measures how the local field structure amplifies coefficient errors into location errors. Unlike $\kappa(\mathbf{A})$, this factor is an intrinsic property of the field and is not under the designer's control. The condition number grows when the Jacobian eigenvalues have very different magnitudes, as occurs near highly asymmetric nodes or saddle points with disparate stable and unstable manifold strengths. In the limiting case, $\kappa(\mathbf{J}) \to \infty$ as $\det(\mathbf{J}) \to 0$, corresponding to non-hyperbolic critical points or near-uniform flow where no isolated critical point exists; the cluster approaches this condition far from the critical point, where the sampled field looks locally uniform. These singular cases are detectable through eigenvalue analysis of the estimated Jacobian and fallback strategies could be developed, such as resizing the cluster.

Across all four error sources, estimation errors shift the equilibrium location of both controllers but preserve the stability predicted by the linear error dynamics. The attraction controller converges to the estimated critical point, and the orbital controller maintains circular motion around it. As a result, sensor noise and conditioning errors affect the accuracy of the final position but not the stability of the control laws themselves.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch6:summary}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
This chapter presented a distributed framework for multirobot navigation in vector fields containing critical points. A Jacobian-based approach used instantaneous measurements from three robots to identify critical point locations without prior field knowledge, and supported an attraction control law and an orbital control law built on these estimates. Simulation across six canonical field types confirmed convergence of these control primitives. In 157 hardware convergence trials, the system achieved a 100\% success rate with average position errors of 0.012~m or less. Orbital control experiments maintained commanded radii within 0.25~m error at small radii and 0.1~m at larger radii.
