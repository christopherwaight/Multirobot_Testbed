\chapter{Cooperative Estimation Framework}
\label{ch:estimation}

The three-robot critical-point estimator of Chapter~\ref{ch:first_order}
and the six-robot separatrix estimator of Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order}
were developed independently, in two papers written a year apart, and
each rederives the same machinery for its own robot count: a formation
of robots reporting simultaneous field measurements, a local polynomial
model fit to those measurements, and a three-layer control architecture
that turns the fitted model into a velocity command. This chapter states
that machinery once, for a fit of arbitrary order $k$, so that the
first-order and second-order cases in the following chapters appear as
two instances of one estimator rather than two unrelated derivations.

\section{Multilayer Control Architecture}
\label{sec:ch4:architecture}
% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex Sec. III (Multilayer Control
% Architecture, Robot Controller Layer, Cluster Space Controller Layer,
% Adaptive Navigation Layer); Draft_11.tex Sec. III-A (Three-Layer
% Structure). Merged into one description covering both the three-robot
% and six-robot instantiations.

Both studies in this dissertation use the same layered control
architecture, established in prior cluster-space adaptive navigation
work \cite{20,40,17,18}. There are three layers. An
adaptive navigation layer estimates the local field and generates a
desired cluster centroid velocity $\dot{\mathbf{p}}_c$. A cluster
controller layer maintains the formation's shape and translates between
cluster-space and robot-space variables. A robot controller layer
converts each robot's velocity command into actuation. Field samples
enter the architecture only at the adaptive navigation layer; the two
layers below it operate on velocity commands and positions alone, with
no reference to the field.

\subsection{Adaptive Navigation Layer}
\label{sec:ch4:nav_layer}

The adaptive navigation layer has three functional blocks. A feature
estimation block fits the local polynomial model of
Section~\ref{sec:ch4:estimation} to the robots' simultaneous positions
and field measurements. A navigation controller evaluates the active
control primitive on that fit and emits a desired centroid velocity
$\dot{\mathbf{p}}_c$; Chapter~\ref{ch:first_order} defines the
attraction and orbital primitives built on the $k=1$ fit, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order} defines the $D$ and $s_1$ trackers built
on the $k=2$ fit. A formation controller regulates the cluster's shape
variables to a target configuration by proportional feedback, holding
the formation geometry the estimator assumes. The navigation and
formation velocity commands combine into one cluster-space velocity
vector, which the cluster controller layer below maps to individual
robot velocities. Swapping the active primitive changes only the
navigation controller; the estimation block and the two lower layers
keep their interfaces.

\subsection{Cluster Controller Layer}
\label{sec:ch4:cluster_layer}

The cluster controller layer maps cluster-space velocity commands to
individual robot velocities through the analytic inverse kinematic
Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_c$, and returns measured robot positions to the
layer above through the forward kinematics, in both directions without
reference to the sensed field. Section~\ref{sec:ch4:cluster_space}
defines the two formations used in this dissertation, the three-robot
SAS triangle and the six-robot pentagon-plus-center; their full forward
and inverse kinematics and inverse Jacobians are given in
Appendices~\ref{app:kin3} and~\ref{app:kin6}.

\subsection{Robot Controller Layer}
\label{sec:ch4:robot_layer}

Each robot's controller receives a velocity command and translates it
into motion; each robot also samples the local field
$\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p}_i) = (u_i,v_i)$ and reports its global position
$\mathbf{p}_i$, supplying the inputs consumed by the estimation block
above. Every robot in both studies is modeled as a single omnidirectional
point mass with a first-order response,
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathbf{v}}(t) = -\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbf{v}(t)
        + \frac{1}{\tau}\mathbf{v}_{\text{des}}(t),
    \label{eq:ch4:lag_cont}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the actuator time constant. For digital implementation at
control period $\Delta t$, discretization yields the momentum update
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{v}[k+1] = \alpha_{\text{mom}}\mathbf{v}[k]
        + (1-\alpha_{\text{mom}})\mathbf{v}_{\text{des}}[k],
    \qquad \alpha_{\text{mom}} = e^{-\Delta t/\tau}.
    \label{eq:ch4:lag_disc}
\end{equation}
Each robot also carries a maximum speed and a stiction floor below which
a commanded velocity produces no motion. This model was identified once,
on the Decabot testbed, and reused unchanged between the two studies
\cite{40,41,39}; the identified values of $\tau$,
$\alpha_{\text{mom}}$, the speed limit, and the stiction floor are given
in Chapter~\ref{ch:tools}. The sensed field enters only the estimation
block of Section~\ref{sec:ch4:nav_layer}: it does not appear as a force
or drift term in Equations~\eqref{eq:ch4:lag_cont}--\eqref{eq:ch4:lag_disc},
so a robot's velocity responds only to its commanded
$\mathbf{v}_{\text{des}}$, never to the field it is sensing.

\section{Cluster Space Control}
\label{sec:ch4:cluster_space}

We refer to a group of robots driven as one unit as a cluster: a
formation treated as a single virtual rigid body whose centroid,
orientation, and internal geometry can be independently specified and
controlled \cite{53,54}. A planar formation of $N$ robots has
$2N$ degrees of freedom, of which three are always rigid-body pose, the
centroid $(x_c,y_c)$ and heading $\theta_c$, and the remaining $2N-3$
describe internal shape. Both formations used in this dissertation fit
this pattern: three robots give six degrees of freedom, three pose and
three shape; six robots give twelve, three pose and nine shape.

\subsection{Three-Robot SAS Formation}
\label{sec:ch4:sas3}
% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex Sec. III-B; Appendix B
% (Three-Robot Cluster Controller Equations), also in app:kin3.

Chapter~\ref{ch:first_order}'s cluster is three robots in a triangular
configuration, parameterized by the Side-Angle-Side (SAS) shape
variables $(p,\beta,q)$: $p$ is the distance from robot 1 to robot 2,
$\beta$ is the interior angle at robot 2, and $q$ is the distance from
robot 2 to robot 3 (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4:sas3}). Together with the centroid
$(x_c,y_c)$ and heading $\theta_c$, $(x_c,y_c,\theta_c,p,\beta,q)$ is the
full six-variable cluster state. The forward kinematics map robot
positions to this state, the inverse kinematics recover robot positions
from it, and differentiating the inverse kinematics gives the analytic
cluster-space Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_c$ used by the cluster controller
layer; the full equations are given in Appendix~\ref{app:kin3}.

\subsection{Six-Robot Pentagon-Plus-Center Formation}
\label{sec:ch4:pentagon6}
% Ported from: Draft_11.tex Sec. III-A (Three-Layer Structure, cluster
% state description); thesis.tex Appendix C Sec. "Six-Robot Pentagon
% Plus Center" (Forward/Inverse Kinematics, Pentagon-of-Pairs).

Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order}'s estimator uses six robots placed at the
vertices of a regular pentagon of ring radius $\rho$ plus one robot at
its center, the minimal symmetric formation for second-order estimation
(Section~\ref{sec:ch4:degeneracy}). The formation controller that holds
this shape parameterizes the six robots not as a hub and five spokes but
as three rigid pairs: pair $k$ ($k=1,2,3$) consists of two robots whose
midpoint is $\mathbf{m}_k$. The three midpoints form a triangle, and the
SAS parameters of that triangle, $(p_1,\beta_1,q_1)$, are defined exactly
as in Section~\ref{sec:ch4:sas3}. Each pair is further described by its
half-length $L_k/2$ and orientation $\psi_k$, with pair 1's orientation
fixed to the cluster heading, $\psi_1 = \theta_c$, by convention. The
full twelve-variable cluster state is
\begin{equation}
    (x_c,y_c,\theta_c,\,p_1,\beta_1,q_1,\,L_2,\psi_2,\,L_3,\psi_3,\,L_4,\psi_4).
    \label{eq:ch4:pentagon_state}
\end{equation}
This pentagon-of-pairs parameterization realizes the ring-plus-center
formation the estimator needs only at the specific target configuration
used throughout Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order}; the grouping into pairs
is a convenience for the formation controller and is invisible to the
estimator, which reads only the six robots' measured positions,
regardless of how the controller parameterizes their shape. The full
forward and inverse kinematics and the $12\times12$ inverse kinematic
Jacobian are given in Appendix~\ref{app:kin6}.

\section{Local Polynomial Field Estimation}
\label{sec:ch4:estimation}

% Main new-synthesis section: states the monomial-basis estimator once,
% for general fit order k, with the first-order (critical-points paper)
% and second-order (Draft_11 / thesis.tex) estimators as instances.

\subsection{Monomial Basis and Formation Matrix}
\label{sec:ch4:basis}
% Ported from: Draft_11.tex Sec. II-A (Local Vector Field Polynomials);
% thesis.tex "Quadratic Fit from Six Simultaneous Measurements"
% (lines 2976-3004); generalized to arbitrary fit order k.

A planar vector field assigns a velocity $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p}) =
(u(\mathbf{p}),v(\mathbf{p}))$ to each position $\mathbf{p}=(x,y)$. Fix a
fit order $k \geq 0$ and expand each component in a Taylor polynomial of
total degree $k$ about a reference point $\mathbf{p}_c$, the cluster
centroid throughout this dissertation. Writing
$\tilde{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}_c = (\tilde x,\tilde y)$,
\begin{equation}
    u(\mathbf{p}) = \sum_{i+j\le k}
        \frac{\partial^{i+j}u}{\partial x^i\,\partial y^j}\Big|_{\mathbf{p}_c}
        \frac{\tilde x^i \tilde y^j}{i!\,j!}
        + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\|\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\|^{k+1}\right),
    \label{eq:ch4:taylor}
\end{equation}
and likewise for $v$, with $\mathcal{O}(\|\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\|^{k+1})$
the truncation error. This is the same expansion the critical-points
paper and Draft~11 each write out independently for their own $k$: a
plane ($k=1$) in Chapter~\ref{ch:first_order}, a full quadratic ($k=2$)
in Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order}. Collecting the monomials of
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch4:taylor} into a basis vector,
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\phi}_k(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}) =
        \left[\frac{\tilde x^i \tilde y^j}{i!\,j!}\right]_{0 \le i+j \le k},
    \label{eq:ch4:basis}
\end{equation}
ordered by increasing total degree, makes the coefficients recovered by
fitting this basis to data equal the Taylor derivatives directly, with
no rescaling. The basis has
\begin{equation}
    n_k = \sum_{d=0}^{k}(d+1) = \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}
    \label{eq:ch4:nk}
\end{equation}
entries: $n_0=1$, $n_1=3$, $n_2=6$, $n_3=10$.
%% NOTE(new-math, checked 2026-09-29): not in any source paper. Equations
% \eqref{eq:ch4:taylor}--\eqref{eq:ch4:nk} generalize the k=1 basis of the
% critical-points paper (Sec. II-B, the plane U(x,y)=ax+by+c) and the k=2
% basis of Draft_11 (Sec. II-A, Eq. for phi(p)) to arbitrary fit order;
% neither source states the general-k form.

Given a formation of $N$ robots at positions $\mathbf{p}_i$ reporting
measurements $(u_i,v_i)$, stacking $\boldsymbol{\phi}_k(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_i)^{\mathsf T}$
row-wise gives the $N \times n_k$ formation matrix $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$,
and the coefficient vectors $\mathbf{c}_u,\mathbf{c}_v \in \mathbb{R}^{n_k}$
solve
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\Phi}\,\mathbf{c}_u = \mathbf{m}_u, \qquad
    \boldsymbol{\Phi}\,\mathbf{c}_v = \mathbf{m}_v,
    \label{eq:ch4:solve}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{m}_u=[u_1,\ldots,u_N]^{\mathsf T}$ and
$\mathbf{m}_v=[v_1,\ldots,v_N]^{\mathsf T}$ collect the measurements. One
matrix factorization of $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ serves both components. When
the true field is exactly an order-$k$ polynomial over the formation's
footprint, Equation~\eqref{eq:ch4:solve} is exact for any nonsingular
$\boldsymbol{\Phi}$; on a smooth nonlinear field the fit carries the
truncation error quantified in Section~\ref{sec:ch4:sensitivity}. At
$k=1$, $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ is the $3\times3$ matrix $\mathbf{A}$ of
Chapter~\ref{ch:first_order}; at $k=2$ it is the $6\times6$ matrix of
Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order}. The critical-points paper's own matrix
$\mathbf{A}$ is built from un-centered robot positions rather than
$\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_i$; shifting the origin to the centroid is an
invertible change of basis on the coefficient space that leaves the rank
of the formation matrix, and hence every degeneracy condition below,
unchanged.

\subsection{Minimality by Fit Order}
\label{sec:ch4:minimality}
% Ported from: Draft_11.tex Sec. II-B (Minimality); cwaight_systems_paper.tex
% Sec. II-C (Minimum Robot Requirements); thesis.tex Proposition
% "Minimality". Generalized to arbitrary k.

\begin{proposition}[Minimality]
\label{prop:ch4:minimality}
Each robot contributes one linear equation per field component to
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch4:solve}, and each component has $n_k=(k+1)(k+2)/2$
unknown coefficients. No algorithm, linear or otherwise, can uniquely
recover the order-$k$ polynomial model of Equation~\eqref{eq:ch4:taylor}
from one synchronous sample of fewer than $n_k$ robots, and $n_k$ robots
suffice exactly when $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ is nonsingular.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With $N < n_k$ robots, $\boldsymbol{\Phi} \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times n_k}$
has a nontrivial null space, so for any candidate coefficient vector
$\mathbf{c}_u$ the vector $\mathbf{c}_u + \mathbf{c}_0$ with
$\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{c}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{c}_0 \ne
\mathbf{0}$, generates identical measurements at every robot; the two
order-$k$ fields are indistinguishable from the data. With $N=n_k$,
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch4:solve} has a unique solution if and only if
$\det(\boldsymbol{\Phi}) \ne 0$. With $N > n_k$ the system is
overdetermined and solved by least squares.
\end{proof}
%% NOTE(new-math, checked 2026-09-29): not in any source paper for general k. Both
% sources prove exactly this argument for their own k: the critical-points
% paper (Sec. II-C) for k=1 ("three equations" for three unknowns), and
% Draft_11 (Sec. II-B) / thesis.tex for k=2. The proof pattern is identical;
% only the generalization to arbitrary k is new.

For $k=1$ this is the minimum-robot argument of
Chapter~\ref{ch:first_order}: each robot contributes one equation to
each of the two $3\times3$ systems of Equation~\eqref{eq:ch4:solve}, so
three robots are necessary. For $k=2$ it is the six-robot minimality of
Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order}: each robot contributes one equation to
each $6\times6$ system, so six robots are necessary. Both chapters give
the sufficiency argument for their own $k$ in full.

\subsection{Degeneracy: Robots on a Common Curve}
\label{sec:ch4:degeneracy}
% Ported from: thesis.tex "Six Robots Are Necessary, and Almost Always
% Sufficient" (Proposition "Conic criterion", Corollary "Pentagon plus
% center", Remark "The center robot is essential"); cwaight_systems_paper.tex
% Sec. II-C (collinearity). Generalized to arbitrary k as one statement.

Nonsingularity of $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ has a clean geometric
characterization at every fit order.

\begin{proposition}[Common-curve degeneracy]
\label{prop:ch4:degeneracy}
For $k \ge 1$, $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ is singular if and only if the $n_k$
sampling points lie on a common algebraic curve of degree $k$, that is,
on the zero set of some nonzero polynomial $q(\tilde x,\tilde y)$ of
total degree at most $k$ (including degenerate curves, such as line
pairs). For $k=0$ the basis $\boldsymbol{\phi}_0$ has a single,
identically nonvanishing entry, so no configuration of robots is
singular.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ is singular if and only if its columns are linearly
dependent, if and only if there is a nonzero coefficient vector
$\mathbf{c}$ with
$\boldsymbol{\phi}_k(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_i)^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{c} = 0$ for
every $i$. The function $q = \boldsymbol{\phi}_k^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{c}$
is precisely a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $k$ vanishing at
every sampling point.
\end{proof}
%% NOTE(new-math, checked 2026-09-29): not in any source paper for general k;
% generalizes the k=1 collinearity statement (critical-points paper,
% Sec. II-C) and the k=2 conic statement (thesis.tex Proposition "Conic
% criterion") to arbitrary k.

\paragraph{$k=1$: a common line.} Three non-collinear robots are
sufficient for the plane fit of Chapter~\ref{ch:first_order}:
$\det(\mathbf{A})$ equals twice the signed area of the formation
triangle, so $\mathbf{A}$ is invertible exactly when the robots are not
collinear.

\paragraph{$k=2$: a common conic.} At $k=2$, Proposition~\ref{prop:ch4:degeneracy}
specializes as follows.

\begin{corollary}[Pentagon plus center]
\label{cor:ch4:pentagon}
Place five robots at the vertices of a regular pentagon of radius $\rho$
and the sixth at its center. Then $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ is nonsingular.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
No three vertices of a regular pentagon are collinear, and five points
in general position determine a unique conic; for the pentagon vertices
that conic is the circumscribed circle. The center does not lie on the
circle, so no conic passes through all six points, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:ch4:degeneracy} applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The center robot is essential]
\label{rem:ch4:center_essential}
Any formation whose six robots lie on one circle, for example a regular
hexagon, is singular regardless of its size or orientation, since the
circle itself is a conic through all six points. Rings are natural
formations for mobile robots, which makes this failure mode easy to
adopt by accident; moving one robot to the center removes it entirely.
This is why the estimator of Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order} uses a
pentagon plus a center robot rather than a six-robot ring.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{$k=3$: a common cubic.} A cubic fit from ten robots is
degenerate exactly when the ten sampling points lie on a common cubic
curve. This third rung of the ladder recovers the third derivatives on
which the true Hessian of the determinant field and the transverse
curvature of an $s_1$ trench depend (Section~\ref{sec:ch4:sensitivity};
Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order}); it has not been built or tested and is
future work, discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:limitations}.

\section{Estimator Sensitivity}
\label{sec:ch4:sensitivity}

The accuracy of the estimator is affected by the conditioning of the
formation, the noise on each robot's measurement, the error in each
robot's position, and the truncation error of the polynomial model
itself.

\subsection{Formation Conditioning}
\label{sec:ch4:conditioning}
% Ported from: Draft_11.tex Sec. II-C "Estimator Sensitivity" (Formation
% subsubsection); cwaight_systems_paper.tex Discussion (equilateral
% triangle, kappa(A)).

The rank of the formation matrix $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ determines whether a
unique fit exists (Section~\ref{sec:ch4:minimality}), and its condition
number $\kappa(\boldsymbol{\Phi})$ indicates how much the fit amplifies
noise. Because the columns of $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ scale with different
powers of the formation radius $\rho$ (a degree-$d$ monomial column
scales as $\rho^d$), condition numbers are reported on the
radius-normalized matrix, each column divided by $\rho^d$; without this
normalization the higher-degree columns shrink as $\rho$ shrinks and a
well-conditioned formation would appear to degrade. The normalized
matrix depends only on the formation's shape, not its size.

This formation-level condition number is a property of the sampling
geometry, and the operator can improve it by choosing the formation's
shape; it is not to be confused with the condition number of a
downstream operator built from the fitted coefficients, such as the
field Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$ whose inversion locates a critical point
(Chapter~\ref{ch:first_order}) or the fitted Hessian used to build a
tracking direction (Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order}). That second kind of
conditioning is an intrinsic property of the field itself, not of the
sampling formation, and is addressed separately in each chapter that
uses it.

\paragraph{$k=1$ instance.} An equilateral triangle minimizes
$\kappa(\mathbf{A})$ among formations of the same size centered on the
sampled region, which is why Chapter~\ref{ch:first_order} uses an
equilateral triangle rather than an arbitrary SAS shape; as the robots
approach collinearity, $\kappa(\mathbf{A}) \to \infty$.

\paragraph{$k=2$ instance.} The pentagon-plus-center formation gives
$\kappa(\boldsymbol{\Phi}) = 8.26$ at every radius. The condition number
grows continuously as the robots drift toward a common conic, with no
threshold at which the fit abruptly fails: moving the center robot out
to $0.99\rho$ places all six robots within $0.6\%$ of a common circle
and raises $\kappa(\boldsymbol{\Phi})$ to $564$.

\subsection{Measurement Noise}
\label{sec:ch4:meas_noise}
% Ported from: Draft_11.tex Sec. II-C (Measurement Noise); thesis.tex
% Lemma "Exact noise gains of the pentagon-plus-center formation".

Each robot carries its own sensor, so the measurement noise on one
robot's reading is independent of the others, and the estimator uses
only instantaneous measurements, so each cycle's fit sees only that
cycle's noise. Noise is modeled as additive on each reading,
\begin{equation}
    u_i^m = u(\mathbf{p}_i) + \eta_i^u, \qquad
    v_i^m = v(\mathbf{p}_i) + \eta_i^v,
    \label{eq:ch4:meas_noise}
\end{equation}
with $\eta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{uv}^2)$. The fit is linear in the
measurements, so the noise reaches the coefficients as
$\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}^u$ and
$\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}^v$; both are zero-mean, so
measurement noise adds no bias to the coefficient estimates. Writing
$\boldsymbol{\Phi} = \bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\,\mathbf{S}_\rho$ with
$\mathbf{S}_\rho$ the diagonal radius-scaling matrix of
Section~\ref{sec:ch4:conditioning} (entries $\rho^d$ by monomial degree
$d$), noise on a degree-$d$ coefficient scales as $\sigma_{uv}/\rho^d$: a
larger formation averages noise better, and it does so more for
higher-order coefficients than for lower-order ones.

\begin{lemma}[Exact noise gains of the pentagon-plus-center formation]
\label{lem:ch4:noise_gains}
For five robots on a ring of radius $\rho$ and one at the centroid, the
coefficient standard deviations of the $k=2$ fit are
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{u_0} = \sigma_{uv}, \qquad
    \sigma_{u_x} = \sigma_{u_y} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{10}}\frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\rho},
    \label{eq:ch4:gain_grad}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{u_{xx}} = \sigma_{u_{yy}} = \frac{8}{\sqrt{10}}\frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\rho^2},
    \qquad
    \sigma_{u_{xy}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{10}}\frac{\sigma_{uv}}{\rho^2},
    \label{eq:ch4:gain_hess}
\end{equation}
independent of the formation heading, and identically for the
coefficients of $v$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The value coefficient is exact: every basis function except the constant
vanishes at the origin, so interpolation forces $\hat u_0$ to equal the
center robot's reading, giving unit gain. The remaining rows follow from
direct inversion of $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ using the fifth roots of unity;
the row norms reduce to the stated constants, and repeating the
computation with an arbitrary ring phase leaves them unchanged.
\end{proof}

These gains are isotropic (independent of heading), so estimator quality
does not degrade as the cluster turns, and they confirm the general
scaling above: second derivatives are harder to sense than first
derivatives by one power of the formation radius.

\subsection{Position Noise}
\label{sec:ch4:pos_noise}
% Ported from: Draft_11.tex Sec. II-C (Position Noise).

A robot with position error
$\boldsymbol{\xi}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_p^2\mathbf{I})$
samples the field at $\mathbf{p}_i + \boldsymbol{\xi}_i$, but the fit is
built from its nominal position $\mathbf{p}_i$. The formation matrix is
therefore wrong by $\Delta\boldsymbol{\Phi}$, whose row $i$ is
$\boldsymbol{\phi}_k(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_i+\boldsymbol{\xi}_i)^{\mathsf T} -
\boldsymbol{\phi}_k(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_i)^{\mathsf T}$, and the fit
returns
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{c}}_u = \mathbf{c}_u
        + \boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\Delta\boldsymbol{\Phi}\,\mathbf{c}_u,
    \label{eq:ch4:pos_noise}
\end{equation}
and likewise for $\hat{\mathbf{c}}_v$. To first order, entry $i$ of
$\Delta\boldsymbol{\Phi}\,\mathbf{c}_u$ is
$\nabla u \cdot \boldsymbol{\xi}_i$, the change in $u$ across the
displacement. This error vanishes in a uniform flow and grows with the
local gradient, so unlike measurement noise it depends on the field. Its
variance is $\lVert\nabla u\rVert^2\sigma_p^2$ for $u$ and
$\lVert\nabla v\rVert^2\sigma_p^2$ for $v$, and the two average, at
$k=1$ or $k=2$, to $\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{J}\rVert_F^2\sigma_p^2$
with $\mathbf{J}=\nabla\mathbf{v}$ the field Jacobian. Combining both
noise sources gives one effective noise level,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{eff}}^2 = \sigma_{uv}^2
        + \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{J}\rVert_F^2\,\sigma_p^2 .
    \label{eq:ch4:sigma_eff}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Truncation Error and Formation Radius}
\label{sec:ch4:truncation}
% Ported from: thesis.tex "Sensitivity: Noise, Truncation, and Formation
% Radius" (lines 3186-3287).

\paragraph{Truncation bias.} If the field is $(k+1)$ times differentiable
with its order-$(k+1)$ partial derivatives bounded by $M_{k+1}$ over the
footprint, the Taylor remainder of Equation~\eqref{eq:ch4:taylor} at
robot $i$ is bounded by a constant times $M_{k+1}\rho^{k+1}$, and passing
it through $\mathbf{S}_\rho^{-1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{-1}$ bounds the
systematic error on each fitted coefficient. At $k=2$ this gives
\begin{equation}
    |\hat u_x - u_x| \le c_1 M_3 \rho^2, \qquad
    |\hat u_{xx} - u_{xx}| \le c_2 M_3 \rho,
    \label{eq:ch4:truncation}
\end{equation}
and likewise for the remaining coefficients, where $c_1,c_2$ are absolute
constants of the formation shape. The highest-order coefficient's bias
decays only linearly in $\rho$: smaller formations track curvature
better, in tension with Section~\ref{sec:ch4:meas_noise}, where larger
formations average noise better.

\paragraph{The radius trade-off.} Combining the two effects, the total
error of the highest-order fitted coefficient is bounded by
$c_2M_3\rho + c_2'\sigma_{uv}/\rho^2$ at $k=2$, minimized at
\begin{equation}
    \rho^* = \left(\frac{2c_2'\sigma_{uv}}{c_2M_3}\right)^{1/3}
        \propto \left(\frac{\sigma_{uv}}{M_3}\right)^{1/3}.
    \label{eq:ch4:rho_star}
\end{equation}
The formation radius is a sensing parameter, not merely a spacing
choice: it should grow with the noise floor and shrink with the field's
higher-derivative scale.

\paragraph{Propagation to derived quantities.} Errors in the fitted
coefficients enter any quantity built from them, such as the determinant
of the fitted field Jacobian used in Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order}. For
two-by-two matrices the determinant expansion is exact at second order,
$\det(\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{E}) = \det(\mathbf{J}) +
\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{J})\mathbf{E}) +
\det(\mathbf{E})$, with $\lVert\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{J})\rVert_F =
\lVert\mathbf{J}\rVert_F$, so the estimated determinant $\hat D$
satisfies
\begin{equation}
    |\hat D - D| \le \lVert\mathbf{J}\rVert_F\lVert\mathbf{E}\rVert_F
        + \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{E}\rVert_F^2,
    \label{eq:ch4:det_perturbation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{E} = \hat{\mathbf{J}} - \mathbf{J}$ collects the fitted
first-order coefficient errors. A first derivative of $D$ inherits errors
only from these same fitted coefficients. A second derivative of $D$ does
not: it involves third derivatives of the field that a $k=2$ fit has no
way to recover, so the fitted Hessian of $D$ carries a model bias that is
independent of the formation radius and cannot be reduced by averaging.
Chapter~\ref{ch:second_order} shows, for the steady double gyre, that
this bias falls disproportionately on the Hessian's eigenvalues rather
than its eigenvectors, which is why a formation too small to resolve
curvature accurately can still track the structure that curvature
defines.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch4:summary}

A single estimator, a local polynomial fit of order $k$ recovered from a
formation of robots reporting simultaneous field measurements, underlies
every chapter in this dissertation that navigates on a field feature.
The coefficient count $n_k=(k+1)(k+2)/2$ sets the minimum robot count,
the formation matrix $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ is singular exactly on a common
algebraic curve of degree $k$, and its conditioning, together with
measurement noise, position noise, and truncation error, sets the
accuracy of every derivative recovered at the centroid. The same
three-layer control architecture and the same cluster-space machinery
carry this estimate into a velocity command regardless of $k$.
Chapters~\ref{ch:first_order} and~\ref{ch:second_order} instantiate the
$k=1$ and $k=2$ rungs in full; Chapter~\ref{ch:limitations} discusses
the $k=3$ rung as future work.
