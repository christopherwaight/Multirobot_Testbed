\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:ch9:limitations}

\subsection{First-Order Estimation and Control}
\label{sec:ch9:first_order}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
Several limitations constrain the current implementation and experimental validation. First, the approach is restricted to planar (2D) vector fields, though many real-world applications involve three-dimensional flows. Extension to 3D fields would require at minimum four non-coplanar robots for Jacobian estimation.

Second, the testbed validates performance only on static fields where critical points remain fixed during operation. The static printed field approach enables precise ground truth validation but does not capture dynamic field behavior.

Third, robot team sizes were limited to three. While the theoretical framework supports arbitrary team sizes through the overdetermined system approach, larger formations present practical implementation challenges including communication bandwidth, computational complexity, and formation control stability.

Fourth, the estimator assumes the field is well approximated by an affine model over the cluster's spatial extent. On fields that are highly nonlinear at the scale of the formation, this local linear fit degrades, biasing the recovered critical point location; the six canonical fields and the two reconstructed hardware fields evaluated here do not probe this regime.

Finally, this work assumes a sampling rate sufficiently high that the system approximates continuous control. The robustness of the approach to lower update rates and communication dropouts was not tested.

\subsection{Second-Order Estimation and Tracking}
\label{sec:ch9:second_order}

% Ported from: Draft_11.tex
Cluster space control is centralized, requires global state, becomes intractable
for suitably large clusters, and is impractical without reliable
high-bandwidth communication among the robots \cite{52}. The claims here
are for small clusters in a limited workspace with ample bandwidth, the
regime the six-robot estimator occupies.

Two limitations sit in the estimator and its band logic. The fit is
ill-conditioned whenever the six sampling points drift toward a common
conic, and no field tested here drives the formation toward one, so the
estimator is untested in the regime its own conditioning limit
describes; the mitigation, monitoring $\kappa(\boldsymbol{\Phi})$ with a
shape-maintenance term, is not implemented. The band test carries no
hysteresis, so noise can chatter the $D$ tracker across its boundary,
which costs speed alone, since both branches share the same transverse
dynamics.

The eigenvector comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:ch7:s1_controller} weakens near the saddles. The residual risk
of a wrong branch under heavy noise is damped but not excluded by the
capture latch.

The $D$ tracker's traversal past a saddle is untested off the benchmark,
and on it runs outside the premise of
Section~\ref{sec:ch7:sep_controller}, as the fitted Hessian is
indefinite where the true one is not.

No controlled head-to-head against the straddle family \cite{29,63,64} or the onboard-FTLE strategy of \cite{8} was run. Running those methods from an off-structure start is outside their stated problems, and their robots are advected while this dissertation's are not, so one simulator cannot host both.
All results are simulated, and the ocean run samples the gridded product without added noise. The ocean agreement is a finding on one time-varying record, where
neither primitive has an analytic guarantee, not a guarantee for arbitrary
flows. Neither instantaneous field is guaranteed to mark the material
transport structure \cite{58}. The no-advection premise is exact for the
planned Decabot follow-on, where the field is encoded and read rather
than physically experienced \cite{28}, but on the ocean field it is an
extrapolation, untested against real advection.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:ch9:future}

\subsection{Time-Varying Fields}
\label{sec:ch9:time_varying}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
The estimation framework uses only instantaneous measurements and carries no state between control cycles. This memoryless property means the estimator does not assume field stationarity, suggesting applicability to environments where the field itself varies with time, that is, where $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p}, t)$ changes shape or strength during operation. This is distinct from a critical point that translates while the field's structure around it stays fixed, a moving-target tracking problem the current formulation does not address. Validating the estimator on a genuinely time-varying field remains future work.

% New text
The Santa Barbara Channel trial of Section~\ref{sec:ch7:ocean} ran the
second-order trackers on a field that changes in time, but on one record
and with no analytic guarantee.

\subsection{Advection-Aware Control}
\label{sec:ch9:advection}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
As noted in Section~\ref{sec:ch2:dynamics}, the robot dynamics (\ref{eq:ch2:continuous})--(\ref{eq:ch2:momentum}) respond only to commanded velocity. In a physical flow such as an ocean current, the field advects the platform, adding a disturbance term that the present controller does not compensate for. Extending the control architecture to account for this direct effect on robot motion is a priority for future work.

\subsection{Higher-Order Fits and Formation Reshaping}
\label{sec:ch9:higher_order}

% Ported from: Draft_11.tex
Ongoing and future work targets a) extending the stability results to
time-varying fields; b) online formation reshaping, to protect the
conditioning of $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$; c) a cubic fit from ten robots off any common cubic curve, which
recovers the third derivatives on which the true $\mathbf{H}_D$ and the
transverse curvature of an $s_1$ trench depend; and d) testing both primitives on the Decabot testbed, then on surface vessels.

\subsection{Three-Dimensional Fields}
\label{sec:ch9:3d}

% New text
The estimator of Section~\ref{sec:ch4:estimation} extends to three
dimensions by counting monomials in $x$, $y$, and $z$. A fit of order $k$
then has $\tfrac{1}{6}(k+1)(k+2)(k+3)$ coefficients per component. A
first-order fit needs four robots and fails when they are coplanar. A
second-order fit needs ten robots and fails when they lie on a common
quadric surface. The sensing and formation control of a
three-dimensional cluster are outside this dissertation.

\subsection{Additional Topological Features}
\label{sec:ch9:features}

% Ported from: cwaight_systems_paper.tex
Beyond critical point detection and orbital control, vector fields contain other topological features of practical interest. Heteroclinic orbits connecting different critical points through their manifolds offer transit routes through complex fields. The Jacobian estimation framework developed here provides the foundation for detecting such features through analysis of eigenvector alignment between neighboring critical points.

\subsection{Testbed Extensions}
\label{sec:ch9:testbed}

% Ported from: IDETC 2025 (DETC2025-167604), Sec. 6, transcribed from the published PDF
The supervised learning calibration technique can be extended to
greyscale sensors for encoding and decoding scalar field monochrome color
maps, creating a unified platform for testing both vector and scalar
field navigation paradigms simultaneously. The modular architecture can
also be enhanced to facilitate controller substitution, enabling
comparative studies between cluster space controllers and alternative
approaches such as swarm-based methods \cite{43}. This modularity
would allow researchers to isolate the effects of different control
strategies while maintaining consistent environmental conditions.
Outdoor testing with autonomous surface vessels \cite{2} and UAVs for
environmental monitoring \cite{46} would examine transferability to
larger scales and real-world deployment challenges.
